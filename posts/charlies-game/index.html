FIFTEEN

How to play

Three players. One card each. You are the only person at the table who cannot see your own card.

The deal

Everyone is dealt one card, face out. Vega and Rook can read yours perfectly. You can read theirs. Nobody can read their own.

So you always know exactly what the rest of the table is holding. The only thing in doubt is you.

The advice

Before anyone decides, each player tells the others whether they should commit to the round or sit it out. You advise both opponents; both opponents advise you. Everyone speaks at the same time and nobody can take it back.

Nothing requires anyone to be honest.

The decision

Commit or fold. You are choosing blind, on their word and whatever you have learned about how much that word is worth.

Folding scores you nothing, but nothing can hurt you either.

The play

Cards turn over and only committed players are counted.

  • If the committed cards total 15 or less, every committed player scores that total. Landing exactly on 15 wins.
  • If they total more than 15, every committed player busts.
  • A joker busts whoever holds it, on its own, and adds nothing to the total. Fold and it cannot touch you.

Card values: ace is 1, jack 11, queen 12, king 13.

Winning and losing

First player to 30 points wins. Three busts and you are out. Busting never costs you points — only a life.

The deck

54 cards: a standard deck plus two jokers. Cards that get committed are set aside and do not come back until the deck runs dry. Cards that get folded, and every joker, go straight back in.

Which means the deck thins out but the jokers never do. The longer a hand runs, the more likely the card you cannot see is the one that kills you. Counting is worth your time.

What to watch for

Advice is not information about the table — you can already see the table. It is only ever about the one card you cannot see, and it is only ever as good as the reason behind it.

Someone planning to commit wants your card in the pot, because a bigger total pays them more — right up until it pushes them over 15. Someone planning to fold has nothing at stake and every reason to walk you into a bust.

Since decisions are simultaneous, you never know which one you are talking to until the cards turn over. The history is there if you want it. What you do with it is the game.

Three properties worth naming

You already know the other two cards

Inverted visibility means you enter every decision knowing precisely what the rest of the table holds. Advice can never tell you anything about the table — only about the single card you cannot see. One unknown, and everything hangs off it.

Advice is honest or poisonous depending on hidden intent

A player who means to commit gains from your card entering the pot, and loses if it pushes the total over. A player who means to fold has no stake in the total at all, and is left with one term in their calculation: the damage they can do to you. Simultaneous decisions mean you cannot tell the two apart in the moment.

The joker is the clean case. It adds nothing, so a joker-holder committing costs the rest of the table nothing and hands them a strike. Everyone who sees your joker will tell you to play. Learning to hear that is the first real skill the game teaches.

The deck gets more dangerous as it drains

Jokers return while real cards leave. Risk climbs on its own, with no designer intervention.

The balance problem

Odds that a committed group lands at 15 or under:

Committed52-card deck40-card deck (A–10)
One100%100%
Two61%85%
All three21%42.5%

A single card can never break 15, so going alone is free and pays about 7. A full table fails four times in five. The correct line is to fold constantly and hope to be the only one in — which produces a quiet game where the table talk has nothing to bite on.

Stripping face cards was proposed and declined for this build. It stays on the register as open question 01 with the full working, because it is the cheapest lever available if playtesting confirms the fold-heavy pattern.

How the opponents think

Vega and Rook are purely self-interested. Neither has a personality, an honesty setting or a scripted bluff. Everything they say falls out of one calculation.

  • Their own card is unknown to them, exactly as yours is to you. They reason over the correct remaining distribution — the live deck plus the card in their hand, minus everything they can see.
  • They estimate what you will do by working out the subtotal visible from your seat, substituting their expected own value for the card they cannot see.
  • They form a private intent by comparing expected value both ways. Bust cost scales sharply with strikes already taken.
  • They advise whatever serves that intent: for each player they run the valuation twice, once assuming that player commits and once assuming they fold, and say whichever produced the better number for themselves.
  • A rival scoring is a cost; a rival busting is a gain. Both are weighted by how close that rival sits to 30, and a player on two strikes is worth much more to knock over.

No deception is written anywhere in the code. It emerges — a bot that has decided to fold has nothing riding on the total, so sabotage is simply what its arithmetic returns.

Your advice moves them

Each opponent holds a private trust value for you, starting even. Your advice shifts their decision in proportion to it: enough to flip a marginal call, never enough to override a clear one. After the cards turn over they check whether following you would have been right and adjust. Mislead them and your influence decays; a run of honest rounds brings it back.

The number is never shown. Reading it off their behaviour is the point.

Interface decisions

The play screen shows the table and nothing else. An earlier build surfaced a live count panel with derived probabilities; it was removed because it did the player's thinking for them and flattened the tension the joker curve creates. The round counter and deck size are the only figures given — enough to count from, if you want to count.

The history drawer is a raw record with no accuracy scores or aggregates. Judging who has been lying is the game, and the interface should not do it for you.
