Operant · Proposal

A cheaper reasoning layer for Fastlane

Prepared for Fastlane. Anish Sharma, Jock Ferguson, Gaurav Thapa 31 Aug 2026

Abstract

You built Fastlane so your customers can focus on their product while their content brings in users on autopilot. Many of your customers, once in equilibrium, might have stable, predictable, and repeatable workflows. Also, their patterns can repeat across many customers.

Your free plan runs the same pipeline as your paid plans, so it costs the same to serve. There are two ways to fix that. Most companies give the free user less, which shrinks the funnel that grew them. You can instead use the extensive usage patterns gained from your paid users to serve free users with a smaller model. This leverages your extensive ball knowledge to actually give you monetary value.

Operant can plug into your observability and auto-learn patterns, and then serve it with a smaller model.

Three actions make model calls cheaper: kv-cache shaping, compression, and learned routing. Each one requires a full team to build. You can have all three, and you build none of them. You will hire a founding engineer soon, and you can give that whole quarter to product instead.

You already build for agents. You ship an API, an MCP server, and a Skill, you just need to change the base url to try it. You also shared a stage with Anthropic, so the Anthropic wire is probably already your wire. It is our native wire too.

We do not know how much of your bill is text and how much is video. So we propose one week in look-only mode. Operant prices your traffic and changes nothing. Then the size of the prize is a measured number and not our estimate.

Methodology

Operant sits in the path between your agents and your model provider. Your provider keys stay yours. Operant encrypts them at rest and strips them on forward.

Each part below states one problem and the answer to it. The facts about Fastlane come from your own public posts and from your site. Please correct if wrong.

1Model calls

Most software companies pay for people and servers. You pay for those, and you also pay a model provider for every unit you sell. Each website you read costs money at the moment of use. So does each script you write and each revision you make after a post. So your gross margin moves with your model bill, and a new customer adds cost on every loop they run.

Operant prices every call and then cuts the bill three ways: kv-cache shaping, compression, and learned routing. A model call costs tokens multiplied by price for each token. The first two actions cut the tokens. The third cuts the price. They are independent, so their effects multiply and do not merely add. Every dollar you take off that bill lands in gross margin, and it lands again on every loop after that.

2Your repeated context is billed at full price

Your agents re-send the whole context on every turn. The brand pack, the site content, the tool schemas, and every earlier turn travel again each time. The new instruction is the smallest part of the call. A provider bills a re-used prefix at about one tenth of the price, but only when the call marks where the prefix ends. Most agents do not mark it.

Operant sees a call that does not shape its cache and places the breakpoints itself. This edit is additive. Operant adds one annotation for each breakpoint. It reorders nothing and it changes no content. A caller that already sets breakpoints stays untouched.

3Stale tool output fills your long sessions

You read whole customer websites into context. On a late turn, most of the input is old tool output that the model already used. You pay full price to send it again on every turn after that.

A rule can mask tool results that are older than a set number of turns. The mask is short and it names what it replaced, so the model can ask for the content again. The mask is a byte-stable text edit, so the compressed form caches as well. Operant compresses only what the cache does not already save. The trace keeps the original bytes, so you can always audit what the provider received.

4The same six or seven goals run 50,000 times on a frontier model

Your inputs change on every run, but your goals do not. We read your pipeline as these steps:

  • read the customer website and get the product, the audience, and the tone
  • match the brand against the trend library
  • write hooks and scripts
  • write captions and post text
  • read the performance results
  • revise, and start the loop again

Each step is a separate goal that repeats across every account. A frontier model serves all of them, including the narrow ones a smaller model could do.

Operant clusters your conversations by goal. For a cluster, it writes the skill that a smaller model needs to do that goal well. It then proposes one rule: this pattern goes to a cheaper model with this skill. On most traffic, one-off goals take about a third of the spend and stay expensive. We expect that share to be much smaller for you, so more of your bill sits in the part we can reach.

5Output quality is the product, so a cheaper model is a risk

A model swap that degrades a hook or a script costs you more than it saves. You cannot accept a change you have not seen evidence for.

No rule changes production traffic until you ratify it. Every learned rule follows the same lifecycle:

  • Shadow. The rule runs beside live traffic and serves nothing.
  • Gate. The gate replays held-out examples and a judge model scores parity against the frontier model.
  • Ratify. You read the score and you approve the rule, or you do not.
  • Demote. One click returns the rule to shadow. The frontier model is always the failover.

Cache shaping and compression change no model and no output, so they carry none of this risk. If you never ratify a single routing rule, those two actions still work.

6Your free plan costs the same to serve as your paid plans

Your free plan is the widest part of your funnel, and your funnel is the reason you grew this fast. Free users run the same pipeline as paid users. They read the same websites and they make the same scripts. So each free signup carries a real model cost and returns no revenue.

There are two ways to fix that. Most companies give the free user less, which shrinks the funnel that grew them. You can instead cut what each loop costs to serve, and leave the funnel untouched. Then you can hold the free plan open at the moment your competitors remove theirs, which is exactly when it is worth the most.

7You cannot see cost for each account

Most companies at your stage know the total model bill for the month. They do not know the bill for one customer, one brand, or one post. Provider dashboards report by API key and by day. They do not report by conversation.

You can have that number. Operant groups calls into conversations and stamps each one with the key that sent it. So you can tell a heavy free user from a light one, and you can tell which paid tier earns money. Then you can price a new plan against a measured cost instead of a guess. The savings ledger states each saving as a counterfactual estimate, and it says so plainly. We would rather give you a number you can argue with than a number you must trust.

8Building this yourself costs your founding engineer a quarter

The work that makes model calls cheaper is real infrastructure. It includes cache breakpoint placement, masks for stale tool output, an eval harness, and a judge model to score it. A full team needs about one quarter to build this well. You are about to hire one founding engineer, and that person will be the most valuable engineering hour in the company for the next year.

You can rent the rest. Model plumbing is solved infrastructure and it is the same for every company that runs agents. You can give that whole first quarter to Blitz Mode, to the trend library, and to the work that only you can do.

One idea we want to try with you

Our gate uses a judge model, because most customers have no better signal. You have a better one. You already measure what performs after a post. Engagement results could grade a routing rule against the real world instead of against a judge. You are the only company we work with that can do this, and we would like to build it with you.

Non-Goals

Limits

Operant is a gateway for text models. It does not touch video or image generation. Your published credit model charges four credits for each image and ten credits for each second of video. So part of your bill sits outside what we can reach.

We make one claim and we make it narrow. Your reasoning layer is unusually compressible, and we can prove that on your own traffic. We do not yet know if that layer is 5% of your bill or 40%. You can find that out quickly, and the next section is how. We would rather scope the claim now than oversell it and lose your time.

Next steps

We propose four steps over about three weeks. You can stop after any step.

  1. A call of 30 to 45 minutes with your engineering side. We ask how you built the pipeline. We ask how the bill splits between text and video. If the text layer is small, we say so and we stop. That answer is worth the call to both of us.
  2. One week in look-only mode. Point one non-production slice of traffic at Operant. Change the base URL and nothing else. Operant routes nothing, masks nothing, and changes no output. It only groups and prices what you already send.
  3. Read the ledger together. We look at three numbers that size the prize:
    • the share of your input tokens that a cache could serve
    • the volume of stale tool output on long sessions
    • the goal clusters that hold the largest spend
  4. Shadow one rule. We pick your highest-volume pattern. Operant writes the skill and runs the rule in shadow. You read the gate score. Then you ratify it if it earns your confidence. Either way you keep the cost visibility from step three.

What we need from you

  • one call with the engineer who owns the pipeline
  • one base URL change on a slice of traffic that is not production
  • your own provider key, which stays encrypted and stays yours

What you get, even if you stop at step three

  • cost to serve for each account, each conversation, and each key
  • the goal clusters that hold most of your spend, named and priced
  • a measured number for how much of your context a cache could serve
